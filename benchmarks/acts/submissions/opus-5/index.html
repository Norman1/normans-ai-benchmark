How to read the map

The lines
  • By land — every one of these that the road data can support follows the surveyed Roman road, not a straight line between dots.
  • By sea — a track found across the coastline itself, so it never crosses land and it rounds the capes a ship would have rounded.
  • On foot, where Acts says someone walked while the ship sailed.
  • Adrift — not a course anyone steered.
  • A direction taken and refused. The bar across the end means they never arrived.
  • Already travelled, earlier in the book.
How much the book actually says

Stated

Implied

Reconstructed

The regions

Roman provinces are drawn as approximate regions, because that is what they were — and because the border data available here is dated about a century and a half after the events. Names in italics inside them are the older countries Acts also uses: Phrygia, Mysia, Lycaonia, Galilee.

The places

Red is where the story is now, dark brown is somewhere it has already been, faint is everywhere else. Click any of them for a card. Modern names are on the cards and never on the map.

Sources & method

Where the geography comes from

Coordinates for the seventy-six places Acts names are from Pleiades (CC-BY 3.0). The coastline, the Roman road network, the province borders and Herod's kingdom are from the Ancient World Mapping Center at UNC Chapel Hill, under the ODC Open Database Licence, derived from the Barrington Atlas. Both were supplied with the brief; nothing here was traced by hand or guessed.

The shoreline is the ancient one. Ephesus, Miletus and Troas were working ports whose harbours have since silted up — Ephesus now sits some eight kilometres inland — and on a modern coastline half the sailing in this book stops making sense.

How the sea routes were found

The coastline is burned into a grid of about eight and a half million cells, roughly a kilometre across, and the sea is flooded outward from open water. Each voyage is then a shortest path across water cells only. That is why no line crosses land, why ships round Cape Lectum and thread the Strait of Messina, and why the run from Patara passes south of Cyprus while the run from Sidon passes north — Acts says which side each time, and the two waypoints are the only thing steering it.

How the provinces were drawn

The bundled border file is bare linework with no names and no closed shapes, so a province cannot simply be read off it. Each one here is grown outward from anchor cities across land only, with a heavy toll for crossing one of the real border lines. Where the data has a border the region snaps to it; where it does not, neighbouring provinces divide the ground between them. The result is an approximation and is meant to be.

What is guesswork, and marked as such
  • Four places Acts names are not in the supplied coordinate set — Phoenix, Lasea, the Forum of Appius and the Mount of Olives. Their positions are approximate and every card says so.
  • Cape Salmone is not guessed: it is taken as the easternmost point of Crete in the supplied coastline.
  • Five land legs are drawn as plain arcs because no road survives in the source data for that ground — Attica and the Peloponnese have none at all. Each says so when you click it.
  • The fourteen-day drift from Cauda to Malta is a reconstruction, and the panel at that point in the story shows the arithmetic.
Dates

The slider moves through the narrative, not through years. Acts gives very few absolute dates; Gallio at Corinth is the firmest, and even the famine under Claudius and Festus replacing Felix are argued over. A slider labelled in years would have to pick one chronology and present it as fact.

The words

There are no quotations from scripture anywhere in this page. Every description of what happens is written for it.
